A summer party, anniversary or classic **on-site company celebration** often saves venue hire and keeps your brand close to home—the car park becomes the stage, the courtyard the terrace, the warehouse your weather backup. The charm: no external house rules, no closing time, full alignment with your company.

To keep a good idea from turning into stress, structured preparation pays off. This checklist walks you through everything to clarify before the first sausage hits the grill and before invitations go out—from compliance and infrastructure to timeline and budget, food, programme and sustainability.

*Note: This is general guidance, not legal advice. When in doubt, involve your local authority, insurer or qualified counsel.*

## 1. The legal baseline—the “party pooper” check

Before music plays and guests arrive, a few formalities belong on the list. They are not red tape for its own sake—they protect the company, neighbours and guests, and lay the groundwork for a genuinely relaxed event.

**Insurance:** Check with your **business liability** policy (or the right cover) whether **private or internal celebrations on the premises** are included, and whether added risks—guests on site, third-party build teams, catering with open flame—are reflected. A short email to your insurer brings early clarity.

**Permits:** If you play **loud music** or erect **tents** above a certain size (often relevant from roughly 75 m²—limits vary by municipality), you may need to **notify or register with the local public order office**. Depending on the setup, a **temporary licence to serve alcohol** can also be relevant. Asking early avoids last-minute stress.

**Performance rights:** When **non-exempt music** is played in public, you usually need to **report and settle** via the relevant collecting society. Even “just a Bluetooth speaker” can matter—clarify the details before the event.

**Fire safety & escape routes:** Keep **escape routes, hydrants and fire-service access clear**. With tents, patio heaters, grills or open flame, **fire extinguishers** and a clear location belong on the list. Many company sites already have a fire-safety policy—a quick check is worth it.

**Food hygiene:** If you cook or serve on site yourself, **basic hygiene rules** apply (cold chain, hand hygiene, separated areas). The easiest route: bring in a **professional caterer** who supplies their own equipment, cooling and hygiene concepts.

**Youth & occupational safety:** Mind **youth protection** when serving alcohol (e.g. if apprentices or family members attend) and provide plenty of **non-alcoholic alternatives**. A well-considered drinks line-up is also a form of care.

## 2. Infrastructure & logistics

Your site is built for operations, not automatically for events. Work through the following:

**Power & water:** Are **connections and capacity** sufficient for catering trucks, cooling, coffee stations or a PA? Higher demand often means **heavy-duty power** or a clean distribution plan via qualified electricians—not something to discover on build day. Note the approximate load per station so the supply can be planned.

**Sanitation:** Are **restrooms** adequate for the expected headcount and easy to reach? If not, plan **rental toilets or trailer units** in good time—including lighting and signage for the evening.

**Parking, access & accessibility:** Where do guests park, and where do **catering vehicles and deliveries** go? Keep routes clear for **emergency vehicles** and provide **accessible entrances** and walkable paths—especially on gravel or industrial surfaces.

**Weather backup:** A **tent**, covered area or **cleared hall** is essential if you cannot shelter everyone in the rain. The alternative is soggy guests and long queues.

**Safety:** **Hazards**—machinery, pits, chemicals, unguarded edges—must be fenced off, signed or made safe for the duration. In short: the guest zone must actually be safe.

**Waste, cleaning & handover:** Plan for **bins, separation and disposal**—and clarify who hands the site back **clean** the next day. A good caterer takes their equipment and dirty dishes with them, so the site is quickly ready for operations again.

**Build-up & breakdown times:** Align **delivery, set-up and tear-down windows** with ongoing operations—including buffers so nothing clashes on the event day.

## 3. Timeline & responsibilities

Great parties aren’t built on the event day, but in the weeks before. **How much lead time you need depends heavily on headcount**—as a rough guide:

- **up to ~100 guests:** around **8–12 weeks** of lead time
- **~100–250 guests:** about **3–4 months**
- **250–500+ guests:** **4–6 months** (more during peak season in summer or December)

Larger events bring more permits (tent, safety/fire concept, possibly traffic/parking), more infrastructure (power, sanitation, furniture) and tighter availability of caterers and suppliers—so plan generously here.

Whatever the size, a rough schedule keeps everyone in sync:

- **Early (2–6 months before, depending on size):** lock in date, budget range and headcount; request caterer, tech and tent if needed; check permits. For larger events, send a **save-the-date** early so the date is set.
- **6–8 weeks before:** **send invitations** and collect RSVPs—earlier for big headcounts, so you get reliable numbers for catering and sanitation.
- **3–4 weeks before:** finalise quantities with the caterer based on RSVPs; confirm weather backup, sanitation and furniture.
- **1 week before:** share the run sheet, site map and contacts; confirm delivery times; do a short site walk-through.
- **Day X:** early build with buffer, brief the team, clear ownership of set-up, tear-down and the clean handover.

**Responsibilities:** Name **one fixed contact** per area (catering, tech, safety, reception). That way everyone knows who decides on the day—and you can celebrate instead of coordinate.

## 4. Budget & suppliers

A realistic budget prevents nasty surprises. Typical cost blocks:

- **Food & drink:** catering, food trucks, beverages, bar
- **Technology:** power/heavy-duty power, lighting, sound
- **Furniture:** tables, chairs, lounge, poseur tables, tent
- **Sanitation & logistics:** rental toilets, cleaning, disposal
- **Décor & extras:** lighting, branding, give-aways

So you don’t have to call ten different companies, a partner with a **regional network** pays off: one contact coordinates catering, bar and—on request—food trucks, furniture or tech. That saves time, coordination and nerves.

## 5. Food & atmosphere

Food is often the heart of the event—and sets how long the mood lasts.

**Catering:** **Food trucks** and mobile stations work well on company sites: less strain on the works canteen, strong **event character**, clear flows. Whether grill, international menus, flammkuchen or a salad buffet—align capacity and timing with your caterer to avoid unnecessary queues.

## 6. Programme & guest experience

A clear thread keeps the mood up without overloading the evening:

- **Team building:** A **mini cocktail class** or hands-on station breaks the ice between departments faster than any round of introductions.
- **Interactive stations:** Live cooking, a DIY bar or a small tasting give guests something to do—and something to talk about.
- **Families & kids:** For a family-friendly format, add non-alcoholic specials, ice cream or crêpes and a small play corner.
- **Dramaturgy:** A short welcome, one highlight (e.g. a bartender show) and a relaxed wind-down give the evening structure.

## 7. Sustainability & reusables

Celebrating sustainably is an expectation today—and good for your image:

- **Reusable over single-use:** quality glasses and tableware instead of mountains of plastic.
- **Regional & seasonal:** short distances for food and drinks, regional partners.
- **Avoid waste:** clear separation, less packaging, sensible quantities against food waste.

Look for **high-quality reusable solutions**: good providers take equipment and dirty dishes away after the party, so the site is clean and ready for business the next morning.

## Pro tip: boost attendance

If the event is framed as **team building** or a fixed summer celebration and takes place **during or right after working hours**, attendance is often much higher than for a purely “extra” weekend slot. Clarity drives RSVPs—and planning certainty for catering and space. A **clear date, a simple sign-up** and an attractive programme are the best advertising for the event itself.

## FAQ: on-site company party

### How early should we start planning?

It depends heavily on headcount: for **up to ~100 guests**, **8–12 weeks** is usually enough; for **100–250 guests**, plan **3–4 months**; and for **250–500+ guests**, rather **4–6 months** (more in peak season)—mainly because of permits, tents, sanitation and the availability of caterers and suppliers.

### Do we need a permit for music or tents?

Often yes: **loud music** may need to be registered with the public order office, **tents** above a certain size as well, and **non-exempt** music usually requires a performance-rights settlement. The exact limits vary by municipality—ask early.

### What if it rains?

Always plan a **weather backup**: a tent, covered area or a cleared hall. That keeps the party independent of the weather and avoids long queues under a small canopy.

### What needs to be available on site?

At its core, just **space and a standard power supply**. Glasses, ice, poseur tables and the complete bar setup are usually brought by a mobile catering service. For higher power demand, heavy-duty power or distribution should be clarified in advance.

### Is cocktail catering worth it for small teams?

Absolutely. In smaller groups especially, a professional bar creates real appreciation and a relaxed conversation you’d never get around a conference table.

## Conclusion

An on-site company party is very doable when **compliance, infrastructure, timeline and hospitality** are thought through early. With clear responsibilities, a realistic budget and a well-planned catering setup, a car park turns into a real event space.
